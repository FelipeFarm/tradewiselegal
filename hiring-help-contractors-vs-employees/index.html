Common hiring mistakes for plumbing companies

Most hiring problems don't start because a business owner is trying to do something wrong. They start because something seemed easier at the time: calling someone a 1099, using an old form, or assuming everyone understands the deal without putting it in writing.

  1. 01

    Calling everyone a contractor

    Calling someone a “1099 contractor” doesn't automatically make them one. If you control their schedule, supervise their work, and provide their tools and materials, the actual working relationship may tell a different story.

  2. 02

    Using the same old forms

    Your business changes as it grows. A handbook or agreement you've been using for years may no longer reflect how your company actually operates.

  3. 03

    Relying on verbal agreements

    Pay, job duties, schedules, and expectations shouldn't depend on everyone's memory of a conversation.

  4. 04

    Not documenting the end

    When someone leaves or is terminated, document what happened and when. A clear record can matter later if the former worker challenges what happened.

Contractor vs. employee for Texas plumbing companies

Correctly classify your team

Misclassifying a worker, even unintentionally, can result in back taxes, penalties, and liability for benefits or protections that should have applied.

The independent contractor vs. employee classification guidelines used by the IRS and Texas Workforce Commission look at the actual relationship: who controls the schedule, who provides tools and materials, whether the work is part of your regular business, and how the worker is paid.

Getting this right at the start of a working relationship is far simpler than untangling it later.

  1. 01

    Independent contractor

    An independent contractor generally has more control over when and how the work gets done, may provide their own tools or materials, and is responsible for their own taxes and insurance.

    If the working relationship qualifies, a written contractor agreement should clearly spell out the scope of work, payment terms, responsibilities, insurance requirements, and independent status. It can also address confidentiality and customer relationships to help protect your business after the job is done.

  2. 02

    Employee

    An employee generally works under more direction and control from your company, including their schedule, how the work is performed, and the tools or equipment they use.

    That relationship also comes with different responsibilities for payroll taxes, wage and overtime rules, and workplace policies. A clear employee handbook helps set expectations around conduct, safety, scheduling, pay, company vehicles and equipment, and disciplinary procedures from the beginning.

Paperwork for your plumbing crew

The right documents from day one

Hiring someone is more than agreeing on a pay rate and telling them when to show up. Whether you’re bringing on an employee or an independent contractor, you need a clear record of the relationship from the very beginning.

The right documents spell out how the person will work with your company, what’s expected of them, and what responsibilities belong to each side. They also give your business something in writing to point back to if there’s ever a disagreement about pay, job duties, company property, or how the relationship was supposed to work.

And the paperwork needs to happen before they start working, not after a problem comes up. Agreements, tax forms, licenses, insurance information, and signed acknowledgments should all be part of a consistent hiring process.

Get it right on day one, and every hire after that becomes easier to document, manage, and protect.

  1. 01

    Contractor agreement

    If they're properly classified as a contractor, put the relationship in writing before they start. Spell out the scope of work, payment terms, timeline, tax and insurance responsibilities, liability, and independent contractor status.

  2. 02

    Employee handbook

    Give employees clear written policies covering conduct, safety, scheduling, pay, vehicle and equipment use, drug testing, and discipline.

  3. 03

    Tax & hiring forms

    Collect the correct paperwork from the beginning, including W-9s for contractors and W-4s and I-9s for employees, along with relevant licenses, insurance, and certifications.

  4. 04

    Signed acknowledgments

    Keep signed agreements, handbook acknowledgments, equipment assignments, and other records together so you have a clear paper trail for every worker.

Good hiring paperwork only works if you use it

Make every new hire easier

Having the right agreements and forms is a great start. But they only protect your business if they’re completed, signed, and kept on file every time you bring someone onto your crew.

A simple onboarding process makes hiring repeatable instead of relying on whoever happens to be handling it that day. Follow the same steps for every new worker, and you’ll have a clear paper trail showing what was collected, signed, assigned, and agreed to from day one.

Three things to handle before they start work

  1. 01

    Get it signed

    Have the appropriate agreement or offer signed before the worker starts, along with any required handbook acknowledgment. Don't wait until there’s already a question or dispute to put the relationship in writing.

  2. 02

    Get it on file

    Gather and verify licenses, insurance, certifications, and the correct tax forms, including a W-9 for contractors or a W-4 and I-9 for employees. Keep everything together in the worker’s file.

  3. 03

    Keep a record

    Keep track of company vehicles, tools, equipment, or other property assigned to the worker. When the relationship ends, document the date and how company property was returned.

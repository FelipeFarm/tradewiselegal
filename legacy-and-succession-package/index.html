Keep things running when you can't

Most plumbing business owners spend years building a company that depends heavily on their knowledge, relationships, and day-to-day decisions. You know the customers, the crews, the jobs in progress, the vendors, the accounts, and all the little details that keep everything moving.

An injury, illness, or other unexpected event can change that overnight. Without a plan, even routine operations like payroll, dispatching crews, accessing accounts, and managing contracts can become difficult when the person who normally handles them suddenly isn’t available.

Keeping the business operating is only part of the issue. You also need a plan for who has authority to make decisions, what happens to your ownership interest, and how your family receives the financial value of the business without being expected to step into a role they may not be legally qualified or prepared to fill.

What happens if you can’t run the business?

  1. 01

    Payroll can’t wait

    Employees still need to be paid when you aren’t there to handle it. Someone needs account access, information, and authority to manage payroll, pay bills, and keep the business’s routine financial obligations moving.

  2. 02

    Jobs still need to run

    Crews need direction, customers need answers and scheduled jobs still need to happen. A continuity plan identifies who can step in, make day-to-day decisions and keep work moving when you’re unexpectedly unavailable.

  3. 03

    Someone has to decide

    If you’re suddenly unavailable, someone may need to access accounts, work with vendors, handle customers and make decisions about ongoing jobs. Establishing that authority ahead of time helps prevent everyday operations from getting stuck.

  4. 04

    Can your family take over?

    Your family may inherit an interest in the company without having the licensing or experience needed to actually run the business. A plan helps protect their financial interest while identifying someone qualified to actually run the company.

  5. 05

    Ownership gets complicated

    With multiple owners, death, disability, divorce, retirement or an unexpected exit can raise questions about ownership and control. Planning ahead establishes what happens to an owner’s share instead of leaving those decisions for a crisis.

  6. 06

    Your business has value

    Years of customers, equipment, employees, and reputation have created something worth protecting. Succession planning helps preserve that value by establishing how ownership and operations transition when you’re no longer able to run the business.

Business succession planning for Texas plumbers

The right plan depends on how you own the business

There isn't a single succession plan that works for every plumbing company. A business with two or more owners has very different questions to answer than a company with one person holding all of the ownership and decision-making authority.

The goal is the same: decide what happens to the business, who can step in and how ownership and operations will transition before an unexpected event forces someone else to figure it out. But the documents and decisions needed to get there look different depending on your ownership structure.

  1. 01

    If you have a business partner

    Co-ownership creates questions that go beyond who will manage the company. If you or your partner dies, becomes disabled, divorces, retires or simply wants out, what happens to that person’s share of the business?

    Without a plan, a surviving spouse could become an unintended business partner, owners can disagree over what the company is worth, or one partner’s unexpected exit can leave the other dealing with obligations and ownership issues neither anticipated.

    A buy-sell agreement addresses those situations in advance. It can establish the events that trigger a transfer, how the departing owner’s interest will be valued, how a buyout will be funded and what happens to management while ownership is changing.

  2. 02

    If you are the sole owner

    When you own the business yourself, there isn’t another owner automatically ready to step in. If you’re injured, hospitalized or unavailable for an extended period, someone still needs to know how to keep the company functioning.

    A business continuity plan can document who has authority to step in and provide the practical information they’ll need, including vendor contacts, account access, payroll processes and details about ongoing jobs.

    A Letter of Instruction can give your family or executor another layer of direction by identifying who to contact, where important documents are located and what needs to happen first. If a trusted employee is critical to keeping the company running, the plan can address that role as well.

What the Legacy & Succession Package covers

Put the important decisions in writing

The goal of owner protection planning isn’t to predict every possible emergency. It’s to make the important decisions before someone else is forced to make them for you during a crisis.

If you’re suddenly unable to work, the immediate concern may be keeping the business running: who can make decisions, access essential information, manage employees, handle payroll, communicate with customers, and keep scheduled jobs moving.

But a longer absence, or death, disability, retirement, or another permanent exit raises a different set of questions. What happens to your ownership? Who has the right to take over? If there are multiple owners, can one owner’s interest be transferred or bought out? And how is the financial value you’ve built protected for you or your family?

The right documents put those answers in writing, so the people who may need to step in have a plan to follow instead of having to figure it out as they go.

  1. 01

    Business continuity plan

    Identifies who can step in if you’re temporarily unable to run the company and gives that person a roadmap for keeping everyday operations moving. This can cover employees, payroll, vendors, customer commitments, ongoing jobs, and other essential responsibilities.

  2. 02

    Buy-sell agreement

    For businesses with multiple owners, this establishes what happens to an owner’s interest after events such as death, disability, retirement, or another exit. It can address when a buyout is triggered, how the ownership interest is valued, and how the transfer will take place.

  3. 03

    Succession plan

    Establishes what happens if you permanently leave the business, whether because of retirement, disability, death, or another long-term change. It creates a path for transferring leadership and ownership instead of leaving those decisions for your family or employees to sort out later.

  4. 04

    Letter of instruction

    Gives the people who may need to step in practical information about the business, including important contacts, accounts, documents, and immediate responsibilities. Think of it as the bridge between the legal plan and the information someone would actually need to carry it out.

When someone else has to step in

Can they actually run the business?

A continuity plan can name the person who should take over, but that doesn’t automatically give them access to everything they’ll need.

Think about how much of your plumbing business may live in your head, your phone, or accounts only you can access. Banking information, payroll, vendor accounts, customer records, scheduling software, insurance policies, passwords and even basic instructions for recurring tasks can become immediate obstacles when the owner is unexpectedly unavailable.

The goal isn’t to hand over unrestricted access while everything is running normally. It’s to identify what someone would need in an emergency, document where that information can be found, and establish the appropriate authority for the right person to use it.

Make sure they have what they need

  1. 01

    Access

    Document where essential business information is kept, including financial accounts, payroll systems, customer records, scheduling platforms, vendor accounts, insurance information, and other tools needed to operate the company.

  2. 02

    Authority

    Access alone may not be enough. Determine who is legally authorized to make financial and operational decisions, communicate with banks and vendors, sign necessary documents, manage urgent business matters, and act on behalf of the company if you cannot.

  3. 03

    Instructions

    Give the person stepping in a practical starting point. Key contacts, recurring obligations, employee responsibilities, active jobs and other immediate priorities can help them understand what needs attention first instead of trying to reconstruct the business from scratch.

Estate planning for Texas plumbing business owners

Protect the owner, too

Owner protection doesn’t stop with documents for the business. Your personal estate and incapacity documents also play an important role in determining who can act for you, who receives your ownership interest, and how decisions are handled if you become seriously ill, incapacitated or die.

Without those documents in place, the people running the business may know exactly what needs to happen operationally while your family is still dealing with questions about who has the legal authority to make decisions or what happens to your share of the company.

Your personal plan and business succession plan should work together so they don’t leave conflicting instructions or important gaps.

  1. 01

    Last will & testament

    Establishes how your property and ownership interests should be handled after your death and names the person responsible for administering your estate. For a business owner, your will should coordinate with any succession, buy-sell, or ownership-transfer plan already in place.

  2. 02

    Financial power of attorney

    Authorizes someone you trust to handle financial matters if you become unable to manage them yourself. This can be especially important when personal finances overlap with business ownership, accounts, obligations, or decisions that cannot simply wait until you recover.

  3. 03

    Medical & incapacity documents

    A Medical Power of Attorney, HIPAA Authorization, and Living Will or Advance Directive establish who can make healthcare decisions, who can receive medical information, and what your wishes are if you cannot communicate or make those decisions yourself.

  4. 04

    Trust & estate planning

    Depending on your circumstances, a Revocable Living Trust and Pour-Over Will can help coordinate how assets and ownership interests are managed and transferred. A Personal Property Memorandum can provide additional direction for certain personal property.

Protect the people and value behind the business

Plan for more than ownership

Keeping a plumbing business intact after an owner’s death, disability, or unexpected absence isn’t only about deciding who owns the company next. You also have to consider the people, knowledge, and financial resources the business depends on to keep operating.

A key employee may hold relationships or operational knowledge that's difficult to replace. Losing an owner or another essential person can create immediate financial strain. And even when the long-term succession plan is clear, someone still needs to know how management and ownership will actually transition. Planning for those pieces ahead of time can help protect the company's value while giving employees, customers, and your family a more stable path forward.

The goal is not simply to transfer a business. It’s to give that business the best chance of continuing to operate and preserving the value you spent years building.

  1. 1

    Key employee retention

    Some employees become critical to the business because of their licensing, experience, customer relationships, or knowledge of day-to-day operations. A retention strategy can help identify those people and create a plan for keeping them with the company during an ownership or leadership transition.

  2. 2

    Key-person insurance

    The death or disability of an owner or another essential person can create an immediate financial gap for the business. Key-person insurance analysis looks at whether coverage may help provide funds to absorb that disruption, replace lost expertise, or give the company time to transition.

  3. 3

    Management & ownership transition

    Knowing who takes over is only the beginning. A transition plan addresses how responsibilities, decision-making authority, management, and ownership move from one person to another so the company isn’t left trying to make those decisions after something has already happened.

Plumbing contracts & customer paperwork

A good agreement protects a good job

A customer calls. You talk through the job, agree on a price, and get to work. It feels simple until you and the customer remember that conversation differently.

Suddenly there are questions about what was included, what materials you agreed to use, why the price changed, how long the job was supposed to take, or whether something should be covered under warranty. And the bigger the job, the more money there may be riding on those details.

Most of the time, the customer isn't necessarily trying to be difficult. They may genuinely remember the conversation one way while you remember it another. But without anything in writing, you're left trying to sort out whose version of a conversation from weeks ago is right.

Good paperwork takes the guesswork out of it. Clear estimates, agreements, change orders, invoices, and warranties put expectations in writing before there’s a problem. Your customer knows what they're getting, what they're paying for, and what happens if something changes — and your business has a clear record of what everyone agreed to.

It takes a little more effort upfront, but those few extra minutes of paperwork can save hours of back-and-forth, protect the customer relationship, and put you in a much stronger position if a disagreement does come up.

  1. 01

    What work is included?

    Clearly define what you are doing, and what is not included.

  2. 02

    What materials are being used?

    Spell out included materials and whether substitutions may be necessary.

  3. 03

    What will it cost?

    State the agreed price and when or why that price could change.

  4. 04

    When is payment due?

    Make due dates, payment methods, late fees, and other terms clear from the start.

  5. 05

    What if the job changes?

    Get additional work and added costs approved before doing the work.

  6. 06

    What does the warranty cover?

    Define what is covered, for how long, and what isn't.

Get it in writing before there is a problem.

The paperwork every plumbing job needs

A plumbing job doesn’t always go exactly as planned. The scope changes, additional work comes up, materials need to be substituted, or a customer remembers the original agreement differently than you do. Without the right paperwork, those small changes can quickly turn into disagreements over the final bill.

Your estimate, service agreement, invoice, and change orders create a clear record from the beginning of the job to the end. They spell out what work was agreed to, what the customer is paying for, when payment is due, and what happens when something changes along the way.

A few extra minutes getting things in writing upfront can save hours of back-and-forth later and put you in a much stronger position if a disagreement turns into a payment dispute.

  1. 01

    Estimates & proposals

    A good estimate should clearly explain the work being performed, materials included, total price, when that price can change, important exclusions and how long the estimate is valid. The customer should know exactly what they're buying before saying yes.

  2. 02

    Service agreements

    Put the scope, price, payment schedule, expected timeframe, access requirements and cancellation terms in writing. Your agreement should also explain what happens when additional work is discovered after the job starts.

  3. 03

    Invoices & payment terms

    Your invoice isn't just a bill. Clearly state when payment is due, how the customer can pay, and what happens when payment is late. Establishing those terms upfront gives you something concrete to point to if you eventually need to collect.

  4. 04

    Change orders

    Unexpected repair? Customer adds something? Code requirement changes the job? Document the additional work and added cost and get written approval before doing it. That's how you avoid “I never agreed to pay for that.”

Don't leave your warranty open to interpretation

Your customer's definition of "covered" might not be yours

Customers hear the word “warranty” and often make assumptions about what it means. Your warranty should clearly explain what's covered, how long coverage lasts, what can void or limit it, and what the customer needs to do if they need warranty service.

Clear warranty terms don't just protect the business. They make difficult customer conversations easier because you're pointing back to something both sides received from the beginning, not arguing about what someone remembers being promised.
